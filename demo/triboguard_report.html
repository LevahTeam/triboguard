Technical report · TriboGuard

Counting What a Plate Cannot Tell You

A treatment that halves a cell population may have killed half the cells or stopped half of them dividing. From averages alone, those are not merely hard to separate — they are provably identical.

Method, results and corrections · birth–death inference, conformal abstention, cost-aware design · 742 tests, all code and artifacts reproducible

1 Summary

Cell-viability assays report one number per well. This work shows that number cannot, even in principle, distinguish cell killing from growth inhibition; identifies the quantity that can; and builds a system that refuses to name a mechanism when the evidence does not support one.

The mean of a birth–death process depends on birth minus death. Two treatments with the same net rate — one killing, one stalling — produce identical average trajectories at every timepoint. The variance between replicate wells depends on birth plus death, so the mechanism lives in the spread between wells, which a bulk assay destroys by averaging.

Four consequences follow, each measured rather than argued. Extra timepoints buy almost nothing. Three wells per condition — the shape of most published dose–response work — pin the birth-to-death split only to within a factor of 146. An endpoint assay such as MTS costs four times the plate of live imaging for identical information. And a conformal guarantee calibrated on one experimental design collapses when applied to another.

2 The problem

A viability assay measures a population property: total metabolic activity, total ATP, total absorbance. A 40% fall in that signal is consistent with 40% of cells dying, with every cell surviving at 60% of its former metabolic rate, and with any mixture of the two. The reading is the same in all three cases.

This matters because the follow-up experiments diverge. A compound that kills is a candidate cytotoxic agent; a compound that stalls division is a candidate cytostatic one, and the two are developed, dosed and combined differently. A paper that reports the fall and names the mechanism has made a claim its measurements do not carry.

3 The identifiability result

Model a well as a linear birth–death process: each cell divides at rate b and dies at rate d, per hour. Starting from N0 cells, the population at time t has

mean(t) = N₀ · exp((b − d)·t)
variance(t) = N₀ · (b + d)/(b − d) · exp((b − d)·t) · (exp((b − d)·t) − 1)

The mean depends on b − d and on nothing else. So for any treatment, another treatment with a different mechanism and the same net rate produces a byte-identical mean curve. No schedule of extra timepoints helps, because every timepoint measures the same degenerate combination.

A sample variance from r wells has r − 1 degrees of freedom. Three wells buy two. This is why replicates are not a statistical nicety in this problem: they are the only channel through which the mechanism is visible at all.

4 Method

The system has four stages, each answering a question the previous one raises.

  1. Estimation. Net rate from the slope of log mean count against time; turnover from a one-parameter regression of the between-well variance onto the model's variance curve. Method-of-moments rather than maximum likelihood, so a wrong number traces to a line rather than to an optimiser.
  2. Calibrated abstention. Split conformal prediction over whole experiments, returning a set of mechanisms that contains the truth at a stated rate. A set of size one is a conclusion; larger sets are the system declining to choose.
  3. Cost-aware design. Ranks extra wells against extra timepoints by information per unit cost, and charges a destructive assay for the wells it destroys.
  4. Failure boundary. Sweeps designs and effect sizes, sorting each into reliable, abstaining, or overconfident.

Simulation is exact rather than Gillespie: a linear birth–death process from n ancestors is a sum of n independent single-ancestor processes with a closed-form law, so a well of 105 cells costs one draw per ancestor per interval instead of one per division event.

5 Results

5.1 The estimator is unbiased, and the uncertainty is the hard part

Over 30 independent datasets at 200 wells, the estimator recovers a true turnover of 0.0600 at 0.0605. It is noisy, not wrong. All the difficulty is in reporting how noisy.

5.2 Wells buy the mechanism; timepoints do not

5.3 What a design can constrain

Because the interval comes from a chi-squared law, its width depends on the well count alone. That makes the question “how many wells do I need” answerable in closed form, and answerable for a study that never reported its replicate count.

5.4 The assay changes the price, not the information

MTS produces its colour change by lysing cells, so a well cannot be read twice and every timepoint needs its own wells. For a three-well, four-timepoint design that is 12 wells consumed against 3 for live imaging — a cost of 13.80 against 4.80 for identical information.

A convenient property falls out of the estimator: it reads a mean and a variance at each timepoint and never follows one well through time. So a destructive assay costs money here but not information, and switching to imaging is a pure saving rather than a trade.

5.5 Coverage holds only within a design

Conformal prediction guarantees coverage when calibration and test data are exchangeable. A three-well experiment is not exchangeable with a thirty-well one, and calibrating on the second while predicting on the first fails in the dangerous direction.

5.6 The failure boundary

Each cell below calibrates on its own design and is evaluated on 80 held-out experiments of that design. A cell is overconfident when coverage breaks, or when the answers the system committed to are wrong more often than the nominal level allows.

Marginal coverage is not conditional coverage. At six wells and effect 0.25 the system holds 0.91 coverage overall — the guarantee is intact — while 75% of the verdicts it committed to are wrong. The wide sets carry the average for the narrow ones. Split conformal promises the first kind of coverage and not the second, and the gap is a real limitation of the method rather than a bug in this implementation.

5.7 When there is nothing to find

Five cells in the map have only one true mechanism — "no effect" — so they cannot test separation and the classifier sets them aside. They test something else, which that label nearly hid: whether the system invents a mechanism from nothing.

6 Case study

The system was applied, frozen, to the study that motivated it: a 2022 report on Tribonema aqueous extract against RAW264.7 cells, measured by MTS. The paper is the case study, not the training data — it contains a few dozen numbers, far too few to fit or calibrate anything, and they are the wrong kind. No rates are estimated from it.

What is assessed is whether the design could carry each conclusion drawn from it. Claims declare what they require, the design declares what it provides, and support is set inclusion.

Two absences are findings in their own right. The report states no replicate count anywhere, and its methods section describes 48-hour incubation where every results section describes 24 hours. Neither is resolved here; choosing one would be inventing data.

At an assumed three wells — the most favourable reading — the birth-to-death split would be pinned only to within a factor of about 146, and narrowing that to a factor of two would take roughly 66 wells per condition.

None of this is evidence against the extract having an effect. The reported effect stands. It is a statement about which further conclusions the measurements can carry.

7 What was wrong along the way

Seven errors were found and corrected during development, each by a measurement rather than by review. They are listed because a system whose purpose is preventing overconfident claims should show its own.

8 Limitations

  • Simulated ground truth. Every result here is measured on simulated wells from the model being fitted. That tests the inference, not the model's fidelity to real cell culture, which has carrying capacity, cell-cycle structure and measurement error the model omits.
  • The boundary is a gradient. Eighty experiments per cell leave neighbouring cells disagreeing — 24 wells at effect 0.70 lands overconfident where 12 wells does not. The map shows a trend, not a cutoff.
  • Conditional coverage is not guaranteed, and §5.6 shows it failing. The system can hold its stated coverage while the specific answers it commits to are unreliable.
  • The case study is design-level. No rates were estimated from the Tribonema data, because MTS does not measure cells. The conclusion is about what the design can support, and nothing here validates or refutes the biology.
  • No external dataset yet. Validating against published longitudinal cell counts with independent live/dead measurement is the obvious next step and has not been done.
